Three lenses on the AI stack

I kept getting lost in competing pictures of the AI stack, so I drew my own. A seller, an engineer, and an investor should draw it differently, because they're asking different questions. The layers follow from the question.

Each layer below shows the real split inside it, framed by that lens's question. Sellers get buyer patterns, engineers get tradeoffs, and investors get bets.

GTM lens

How do buyers buy at this layer, and where do deals stall?

Selling AI infrastructure now means passing a test you can't see. The buyer builds an evaluation harness: a repo with production-like data, representative prompts, and the models underneath. They let it run and pick whichever vendor comes out ahead.

The harness is a fourth party in the deal, next to you, the buyer, and your competitor. Buyers often won't let you see inside it, even when they're on your side.

So this lens sorts the stack by buying behavior. It gives retrieval and harnesses their own layers because that's where the newest deals get decided.

The buyer's eval harness isn't one of the layers. It sits across every infrastructure layer below the apps, and it decides most of these deals.

Vertical and functional apps

Domain software sold to a department: legal, finance, clinical, support.

  • Harvey
  • Rogo
  • Profound
  • Abridge
  • Sierra

Buyers split on

Seats

Per-user licenses, the SaaS default. The budget is predictable, and the vendor eats the compute cost of its heaviest users.

Usage or outcomes

Price follows what gets done. Sierra charges per resolved conversation, and GitHub Copilot moved to usage billing on June 1, 2026. Price tracks value and compute, but the buyer loses a fixed budget.

The second buying question is whose budget it is: the department that feels the pain, or IT.

Retrieval and real-time web data

Web search, extraction, and research built for agents rather than people.

  • Parallel
  • Exa
  • Tavily (Nebius)
  • Firecrawl

Buyers split on

Embeddings-first

Exa searches by meaning. It offers Find Similar (give it a URL, get related pages back) and keeps its own indexes of people and companies.

Objective-first

Parallel takes a plain-language objective alongside keyword queries, returns excerpts shaped for LLMs, and prices each request by how deep you want it to dig.

The two have converged: both cover search, extraction, research, and monitoring. Each publishes benchmarks it wins, and a third-party test reversed one of them. That's why the buyer trusts only their own harness. Tavily was acquired by Nebius in February 2026 and still runs under its own brand.

Harnesses, gateways, and switchboards

The layer that makes models and tools usable inside a company: connectors, routing, and policy.

  • Runlayer
  • OpenRouter
  • Kong
  • Portkey

Buyers split on

Build time

Get the connector right before anything runs. Generate the SDK or MCP server from the API spec. Stainless did this for most of the big labs until Anthropic bought it in May 2026.

Run time

Govern what agents do once they're live: which tools they call, what data they touch, what it costs. Gateways like Runlayer and Kong sit here.

It's the same split as Chainguard and Wiz in cloud security: prevent problems at build, or catch them at run. The buyers differ too. A gateway is bought centrally by a platform or security team. OpenRouter is bought by one developer with a credit card who wants one key for many models.

Inference

Companies that run models for you and sell the compute.

  • Together AI
  • Fireworks AI
  • Modal
  • Baseten

Buyers split on

Serverless, per token

Pay per token for open models. Bought off a latency benchmark, and swapped out when a better benchmark shows up.

Dedicated capacity

Reserved deployments, or per-second compute for your own code. Signed like infrastructure, with a capacity commitment.

Same layer, very different churn. Per-token customers switch on the next benchmark. Capacity customers renew.

Foundation model labs

Frontier models and reasoning.

  • OpenAI
  • Anthropic
  • Google (Gemini)
  • xAI

Buyers split on

Direct from the lab

New features arrive first, but it's a separate contract and a separate security review.

Through your cloud

The same model through Bedrock, Vertex, or Azure. Spend counts against the cloud commit the company has already signed, and procurement is already done.

For many enterprises, the default model is whichever one their existing cloud contract makes easiest to buy.

Builder lens

What am I trading to get what?

Engineers debug systems that don't give the same answer twice. When an agent fails in production, they need to know which piece failed: a stale document, a bad lookup, a prompt that regressed, or a slow model.

So this lens breaks the stack into parts you can test and swap on their own. Each layer is a trade, and it's usually finer-grained than speed versus cost versus risk.

Frontend

Where the tokens show up on screen.

  • Vercel (Next.js, AI SDK)
  • Streamlit
  • Gradio

You trade

Production UI

The Vercel AI SDK on Next.js: streaming, JavaScript, built to face customers.

Python demo

Streamlit (owned by Snowflake) or Gradio: an internal tool in an afternoon. Not built for polish.

Speed to the first demo, against what a customer eventually sees.

Tool access

How agents reach real systems: CRMs, databases, internal APIs.

  • Runlayer
  • Kong
  • MCP servers

You trade

Central gateway

One place to set policy and log every call. The cost is one more hop, and one more thing that can go down.

Direct connections

Each agent wires up its own tools. Fastest to build, but you get sprawl and no single audit trail.

This is where agents fail in new ways: wrong permissions, runaway tool calls, costs nobody expected.

Orchestration

The control flow: tool-calling loops, state, and handoffs between agents.

  • LangGraph
  • CrewAI
  • LlamaIndex
  • OpenAI Agents SDK
  • Claude Agent SDK

You trade

Explicit graph

LangGraph: you draw the control flow, loops included. You can debug it, but you write more code.

Role-based crews

CrewAI: define agents and their roles, then let them work it out. Fast to start, with less control.

The vendor's own loop

The model provider's agent SDK. The least code of the three. It bets that models keep improving, and it ties you closer to one vendor.

LangGraph graphs can cycle, because agents loop. Calling them DAGs is wrong: a DAG can't loop.

Context and retrieval storage

Vector search, keyword search, and graphs that assemble context for the prompt.

  • pgvector
  • Pinecone
  • Qdrant
  • Chroma
  • turbopuffer
  • Neo4j

You trade

Vectors in Postgres

pgvector: one fewer system to run, and vectors stay consistent with the rows they describe.

A dedicated engine

Pinecone or Qdrant: more scale and better filtering, plus another system to keep in sync.

HNSW is a search algorithm that both sides use, not special hardware. So the real choice is operational. turbopuffer adds a third option: keep vectors in cheap object storage and accept slower cold queries. Neo4j makes relationships explicit, but someone has to build and maintain the graph.

Model serving

The runtimes that turn GPUs into tokens.

  • vLLM
  • SGLang
  • TensorRT-LLM
  • Ollama

You trade

vLLM

The default. The broadest model support, and high throughput from paged memory and continuous batching.

SGLang

Prefix caching pays off when many requests share a long prompt, which is exactly what agents do.

TensorRT-LLM

The most speed on NVIDIA hardware, paid for with portability.

Ollama

One command on a laptop. Not built for many users at once.

Throughput, latency, portability, and convenience rarely come in one package.

Data ingestion and storage

Getting internal data into shape before any model sees it.

  • Fivetran
  • dbt
  • Databricks
  • Snowflake
  • Unstructured

You trade

Lakehouse on open formats

Databricks on open table formats like Iceberg. You own the format and can swap query engines, but you run more of it yourself.

Managed warehouse

Snowflake: one vendor runs storage and compute for you.

The two have converged on Iceberg, so the line is blurrier than it was. The trade that holds steady is ELT: land raw data with Fivetran, then transform it in SQL with dbt, with tests and lineage. You give up some freshness to get auditability. Unstructured handles the PDFs and documents that SQL can't.

Alongside every layer

Observability, evals, and guardrails

Runs alongside every layer rather than sitting in the stack. It traces, scores, and alerts.

  • Braintrust
  • LangSmith
  • Arize Phoenix
  • Helicone

You trade

Eval-first

Braintrust meters scores. Its question is "is this change better?" Its Pro plan has unlimited users.

Trace-first

LangSmith meters traces. Its question is "what happened in this request?" It's priced per seat, plus usage.

They overlap more than the marketing suggests. Braintrust even ships a wrapper that sends the same traces and evals to both. Arize Phoenix is the pick when open source, OpenTelemetry, or self-hosting matters. Helicone works as a proxy: change one base URL and you're done, but it now sits in the path of every request.

Investor lens

What am I underwriting, and who ends up buying whom?

Investors ask where margin collects and how each company exits. Operational detail rarely changes that answer, so this lens uses fewer, wider layers.

Each layer is a bet. The clearest evidence for which bets are working is who bought whom.

Applications

End-user software that owns the workflow and the customer.

  • Cursor
  • Harvey
  • Perplexity
  • Midjourney

You underwrite

Rent the model

Route each task to the cheapest model that can do it, and ride the price drops.

Own the model

Train your own to control cost of goods. Cursor's Composer is the example.

Inference sits in cost of goods, so margins start below software norms. ICONIQ projects AI product gross margins around 52% in 2026, up from 41% in 2024, against 75 to 85% for traditional SaaS. The open question is whether that climb continues or the gap is permanent. Midjourney is the counterexample in this lens: it grew without venture money.

Tooling and middleware

The developer middleware used to assemble apps.

  • LangChain
  • LlamaIndex
  • Pinecone
  • Weights & Biases

You underwrite

Open source first

Win developers for free, then charge for the hosted platform. LangChain and LlamaIndex.

Closed from day one

Charge from the first customer and sell the whole workflow. Braintrust.

Is this a company or a feature? The 2026 exits say acquisition by the layer above or below. Stainless went to Anthropic (reported above $300M), which then shut down the hosted product. Tavily went to Nebius ($275M up front, up to $400M on milestones). Weights & Biases went to CoreWeave in 2025.

Models

Closed frontier labs and open-weight models.

  • OpenAI
  • Anthropic
  • Google
  • Meta Llama
  • Mistral
  • DeepSeek

You underwrite

Closed frontier

Charge a premium for being ahead, and bet the lead lasts long enough to pay back each training run.

Open weights

Give the model away to cheapen what others sell, and make money somewhere else. The old "commoditize your complement" play.

a16z estimates that the cost of inference at a given level of performance falls about 10x a year. The bet is that usage grows faster than prices fall.

Compute and infrastructure

Chips, data centers, and GPU rental.

  • NVIDIA
  • TSMC
  • CoreWeave
  • Nebius
  • Lambda
  • The hyperscalers

You underwrite

Asset-light

NVIDIA designs the chips and TSMC makes them. It's a margin business.

Asset-heavy

Neoclouds buy GPUs, often with debt, and rent them out. It's a utilization business, and the valuation turns on how many years a GPU stays useful.

"Pick-and-shovel sellers win regardless" holds for NVIDIA. For neoclouds it depends on utilization, and they're buying their way up the stack, as with Nebius and Tavily or CoreWeave and Weights & Biases.
