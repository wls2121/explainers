101 · Foundations

Why version-controlled SQL isn't enough

Refs, tests, and modular models are real and valuable — but they buy you trusted tables. The gap opens the moment someone turns a table into a number. Four steps to see exactly where.

1. A trusted table is not a trusted metric

The whole thing in one sentence. dbt models give you trusted tables. A metric is not a table — it's a table plus an aggregation plus a set of dimensions nobody has chosen yet.

When you write fct_orders with refs and tests, you've earned trust in a specific set of rows at a specific grain. Real and valuable. But "revenue" isn't rows. It's SUM(order_total) — sliced by whatever dimension the asker wants, at whatever grain, joined across whatever tables carry those dimensions. Your trust stops at the edge of the table. The instant someone aggregates it, they're writing SQL you never reviewed.

An analyst has two honest ways to get their number. Pre-build every version as a table (§2), or write the GROUP BY themselves against your model (§3–4). Both break — each in a way version control can't catch.

2. Pre-building fails: the combinatorial explosion

Say 12 metrics, each sliceable by 8 dimensions, at 4 grains. Pre-materialize every combination someone might group by and you're not maintaining 12 tables — you're maintaining a table per subset of dimensions. For 8 dimensions that's 2⁸ = 256 groupings, × 12 metrics × 4 grains = 12,288 models. Nobody builds and tests that. This is the old OLAP cube: precompute the combinations, drown in them.

So the analyst does the sensible thing instead: writes the GROUP BY by hand against your trusted fct_orders. That's where trust actually breaks — in two mathematically specific ways.

3. Ad-hoc fails, part 1: fan-out

Fan-out is what happens when you aggregate a column after a one-to-many join, and the join quietly duplicates your rows before you sum them.

Two tables. orders (revenue, at order grain) and order_items (many per order):

True revenue = 300.

Order 1 → 2 items. Order 2 → 3 items.

"Revenue by item" — join and sum. Reasonable-looking, version-controlled, refs and all:

select o.region, sum(o.order_total) as revenue
from orders o
join order_items i on i.order_id = o.order_id
group by o.region

The join duplicates order_total before the sum — order 1's 100 lands on 2 rows, order 2's 200 on 3 rows:

regionorder_idorder_total (post-join)
West1100
West1100
East2200
East2200
East2200

Returns West = 200, East = 600, total = 800. Truth is 300. Inflated 2.67×, silently, no error, no failed test. Your model was perfect. The question broke it.

Why refs don't save you

Version control protects the definition of the table. It does nothing about the aggregation someone writes on top of it in Looker, in Hex, in an ad-hoc query. Fan-out is a property of the read, not the model — there's no test on fct_orders that fires when it's summed across the wrong grain.

4. Ad-hoc fails, part 2: you can't average an average

Fan-out's quieter cousin. Ratios and averages are non-additive — you can't combine sub-totals by averaging them. Conversion rate = orders ÷ sessions:

dayorderssessionsconv. rate
Mon101,0001.0%
Tue901,0009.0%

Average the daily rates: (1.0% + 9.0%) / 2 = 5.0%. Correct at the combined grain: (10 + 90) / (1,000 + 1,000) = 5.0% — matches only because the denominators are equal. Now weight the days unevenly — Monday 10,000 sessions at 1% (100 orders), Tuesday 1,000 at 9% (90 orders). Averaging the rates still says 5.0%, while the truth is (100 + 90) / (10,000 + 1,000) = 1.7%. Off by ~3×.

A ratio must be stored as its two parts — numerator and denominator, each additive — and divided only at the final grain of the question. A pre-built conversion_rate column can't do that: once it's a rate, it's forgotten its denominator.

102 · The layer itself

What a semantic layer actually is

You wanted the line drawn cleanly: where do trusted tables stop and the semantic layer begin, and why doesn't the dimensionality mess just move upstream? Here.

5. The line: it sits on your tables, it doesn't replace them

Cleanest way to hold it: a dbt model is SQL that builds a table. A semantic model is YAML that describes a table you already built. It materializes nothing. Zero new rows. It's an annotation layer that tags an existing model: "in fct_orders, order_id is the grain, customer_id is a join key, order_total is a number you're allowed to sum."

Do semantic models have to be 1:1 with tables?

Essentially yes — in MetricFlow, one semantic model wraps one dbt model. It does not merge tables or pre-join anything. So the boundary is clean and easy to hold in your head: models = the tables and their trust (SQL, refs, tests, version control); the semantic layer = a per-table description of how to aggregate and join them (YAML). SQL builds. YAML describes. The many-dimensional freedom doesn't come from one fat semantic model — it comes from composing many 1:1 semantic models at query time (§6).

6. Why the dimensionality problem doesn't move upstream

This is the crux you flagged. The combinatorial explosion in §2 only exists if you try to materialize combinations. The semantic layer's move is to never materialize them — keep your tables normalized and atomic, and assemble at read time.

Concretely: keep fct_orders at order grain, keep dim_customer (region, segment, …) as its own table. Don't pre-join them. Each gets its own 1:1 semantic model, and they share the customer entity — a foreign key in the fact, the primary key in the dimension. That shared entity is the join path, declared once.

The answer to "doesn't it just move upstream?"

No — it's dissolved by deferral, not relocated. The 12,288 combinations aren't managed upstream (you didn't pre-join) or downstream (no analyst writes the joins). They're generated one at a time, on demand, from normalized tables. Contrast the two classic escapes, which both push the problem into materialized tables: One Big Table (pre-join everything wide, rebuild constantly, still stuck at one grain) and cubes (pre-aggregate, explode). The semantic layer is neither: stay normalized, assemble at read.

7. Two faces: how you write it vs. how tools use it

You put your finger on the axis that clears most of the fog. "Semantic layer" names two different things people run together — a specification you author, and a query engine tools call. Keep them apart:

Write side — the spec

you authorgit

YAML in your dbt project (or LookML in Looker). Static text, version-controlled, reviewed in PRs, living next to the models it describes. Declares entities, dimensions, measures, metrics. This is the human-owned definition.

Serve side — the engine + API

tools callruntime

MetricFlow compiles the spec; an API (JDBC / ADBC / GraphQL) serves it. A tool sends "metric + group-by," the engine generates warehouse SQL and returns rows. No tool ever writes a join.

Write side. The spec is two files. The semantic model — where join keys and grain get declared once:

# models/semantic/orders.yml  — describes fct_orders, builds nothing
semantic_models:
  - name: orders
    model: ref('fct_orders')          # 1:1 with the dbt model
    defaults: {agg_time_dimension: order_date}
    entities:
      - {name: order,    type: primary, expr: order_id}
      - {name: customer, type: foreign, expr: customer_id}  # join path
    measures:
      - {name: revenue, agg: sum, expr: order_total}       # grain = order
    dimensions:
      - {name: order_date, type: time}

And the metric — defined one time, for everyone. Note the ratio keeps its two parts (the §4 fix, encoded):

# models/semantic/metrics.yml
metrics:
  - {name: revenue, type: simple, type_params: {measure: revenue}}
  - name: conversion_rate
    type: ratio
    type_params: {numerator: orders, denominator: sessions}

Serve side. A consumer asks for the metric plus the dimensions it wants. The join path from the spec above is already known, so no joins, no GROUP BY, no chance of fan-out:

# a GraphQL client hitting the Semantic Layer API — no SQL, no joins written
{ query(metrics: ["revenue"], groupBy: ["customer__region", "metric_time__month"]) {
    region  month  revenue        # computed at the correct grain, server-side
} }

Same definition answers Looker, Hex, a spreadsheet, and an AI agent — because it's served over an API, not trapped in one BI tool. That portability is the practical difference from LookML, where the definition lives inside Looker; and the grain-awareness (aggregate-then-join) is what makes the fan-out bug structurally unwriteable rather than something you hand-guard with symmetric aggregates.

8. One definition, every tool

Without that middle box, "active customer" gets redefined in LookML, again in a Hex notebook, again in the exec's hand-written SQL. Three definitions, three numbers, one bad Monday. The refs were never the problem — the metric lived nowhere canonical, so everyone rebuilt it. The semantic layer is that canonical home, served by API so Looker, Hex, and an agent all return the identical number.

201 · The map above the metrics

Ontology

The semantic layer computes numbers correctly. An ontology is the bigger thing an AI needs: a map of what exists and how it connects. Where that line falls — and where you'd actually author one — is genuinely unsettled, so this tier is part definition, part frontier.

9. The seam exposed: why a perfect DAG still fails AI

If you pitch an ontology to a sharp data engineer by saying "the semantic layer can't hold this relationship," you lose the room — they'll design a bridge table in their head to prove you wrong, and they'll be right. So drop that argument entirely.

The real seam isn't whether the data is missing from the warehouse. It's whether an AI agent can infer the intent of a question from a flattened, tabular model. Here's what breaks when your data model is flawless and you still don't have an ontology.

1. The "which column?" problem — intent resolution

You anticipated corporate hierarchies perfectly. Your semantic layer exposes account_name, subsidiary_name, and global_parent_name as dimensions on revenue. The user asks the agent: "What's the revenue for Disney?"

Semantic layer alone

The agent sees three valid ways to group by "Disney" and takes a literal guess. Picks account_name = 'Disney' and misses ESPN and Pixar — or picks global_parent_name when the user only wanted the theme-park entity. Perfect math, wrong question.

With an ontology

The graph intercepts the intent: "Disney exists at both the parent and subsidiary level — ask the user to disambiguate before you query." It governs which column is right for the context.

The failure isn't in the warehouse; it's in the agent's reasoning. The ontology governs that reasoning — and this holds even when your data model is flawless, which is why the bridge-table rebuttal doesn't touch it.

2. The MagSafe edge — the intuition pump

A user asks: "If we discontinue the iPhone 14 Pro, how much accessory revenue is at risk?" A determined engineer will point out they can build a product_compatibility bridge table — and they can. But a DAG is built for hierarchies (parents and children); the physical world is a web.

Expecting an LLM to reliably infer [MagSafe Case] —IS_COMPATIBLE_WITH→ [iPhone 14 Pro] from a flattened bridge table is a hallucination waiting to happen. An ontology maps that web natively and the agent just traverses the edge.

3. As-of relationships — the kill shot

Disney bought 21st Century Fox in March 2019. A user asks: "What was Disney's revenue in 2018?"

A good semantic layer handles the date math fine — the agent asks for an "as-of 2018" number and the layer does the temporal join. The hard part isn't the date math. It's as-of relationships.

"Who did Disney own in 2018?" is a temporal question about an edge, not a value — and neither a plain semantic layer nor an LLM traverses time-varying relationships cleanly. Modeling ownership as [Disney] —OWNS→ [Fox] {from: 2019-03-20} turns the agent's "was this true then?" into a lookup instead of a reasoning task it will almost certainly get wrong. (The genuinely nasty version is bitemporal — when we learned it was true vs. when it became true — which an edge with two date-pairs handles and SCD2 handles badly.)

The golden rule

The semantic layer says: "here are the columns you can group by, and how the math works." The ontology says: "here's how those concepts interact in the real world, so you know which columns to group by in the first place."

Commercially, that fork — can the semantic layer's map cover enough for the agent to reason correctly, or do you need a real graph beside it — is what every "ontology layer" evaluation is actually deciding.

"Is it writeback?"

No. Both the semantic layer and the ontology are read / definition layers — they tell tools how to compute and interpret data, and push nothing back into a source system. Writeback / reverse ETL (Hightouch, Census, Looker write actions) moves modeled data out to Salesforce or an app — opposite direction. If someone frames the ontology as "writeback," pin down what they mean; they may be conflating "one governed place everything reads from" with "writes data somewhere."

10. Where an ontology actually lives (and is it git?)

You spotted the real question by following the artifact ladder. Each layer has a home:

LayerWritten asLives in
Modeldbt SQL (.sql)git — builds a table
Semantic layerYAML / LookMLgit — describes the table
Ontology…less settledsee below

There's no single dominant standard for the third row yet — that's the honest state of it. In practice it takes one of three forms:

1. Today, for a dbt shop, your ontology mostly is your dbt project. Models + semantic models + relationships tests + description/meta fields already encode "what connects to what." The entity graph and foreign-key relationships are a lightweight, working ontology — and it's git-native, which is exactly your instinct. An agent that can read that project already has a real map. Most teams reaching for an "ontology layer" for AI want this, extended — richer descriptions and relationships in the same YAML — before they need anything heavier.

2. A formal ontology, from the semantic-web world. RDF triples and OWL, authored in Turtle (.ttl) files, stored in a triple store or graph database (Neo4j, GraphDB). Typed classes, relationships, and inference rules an agent can traverse and reason over. More expressive, heavier, separate tooling from your warehouse. The .ttl can live in git; the materialized graph lives in the graph store.

3. A vendor knowledge-graph / metrics-catalog layer that sits above the warehouse and stitches definitions into a graph for you.

The frontier, stated plainly

For a git-native team, the ontology starts life in git, as an extension of the same project that holds your models and semantic YAML — descriptions, entities, and relationships an agent can read. The open question isn't "git or not"; it's when plain-text descriptions and relationships stop being enough and you need a real typed graph with inference. Most teams are nowhere near that line yet — which is why "can the dbt semantic layer be our ontology?" is a live, winnable question rather than a settled no.

11. Is "ontology" a real category, or context engineering finding a name?

Fair skepticism, because the word is doing a lot of work right now. Here's the honest state of it in 2026.

It's further into becoming a real category than the hype-cycle reflex suggests. There's a named market (analysts size "agentic AI + semantic layer + knowledge graph" around $1B in 2026, growing ~25% a year), a stable of vendors selling it, and concrete product moves — Neo4j shipped an "Aura Agent" with ontology-driven agent construction and hosted MCP in early 2026. The thing reviving a decades-old idea is agents: a dashboard tolerates a fuzzy definition because a human reads it, but an agent that's about to act needs governed context, typed relationships, and traceable logic first. That's the same pressure that made the semantic layer a category over ~2.5 years, now one level up.

What's not settled is the architecture — and that's the clash you're feeling. Three worlds are converging on one 2026 problem ("how does an agent get the right business answer, not a plausible-wrong one"), and they don't share a vocabulary:

The semantic layer became a category because it earned a crisp, provable one-sentence job (consistent, fan-out-safe metrics). "Ontology" is being asked to do three jobs at once — route agents, define business entities, govern access — so it hasn't fully set. The near-term pattern that's emerging: "context engineering" is the practice people actually do; "ontology / knowledge graph" is the product category hardening underneath it; the semantic layer is the substrate both stand on. The real fight is over which architecture wins:

Graph-native stack

A new layer above the warehouse — RDF/OWL, a triple store, a reasoning engine. Palantir, Stardog, Neo4j. More expressive; typed relationships and inference. Heavier, separate tooling.

Semantic-layer extension

Warehouse-native — descriptions, entities, relationships added to the semantic layer you already have, exposed over an API/MCP. Lighter, git-native, no new store. Bet: "good enough" meaning without a graph DB.

That fork — new stack vs. extension — is the live commercial question every "ontology layer" evaluation is actually deciding. Neither has clearly won.

12. The real ontology stack — and what an "ontologist" does

Yes, "ontologist" is a real job, and it long predates the AI wave — it comes from the semantic-web world, not analytics. The clearest one-liner: an ontology is "a formal, explicit specification of a shared conceptualization of a domain." An ontologist doesn't record facts; they define the schema of meaning — the types of things that exist, how they relate, and the rules and constraints that govern them. "A return is a kind of transaction; every return must reference exactly one order; a customer becomes inactive after 18 months with no order." Then a machine can infer new facts from those rules, not just look them up.

One distinction that clears a lot of confusion: the ontology is the schema; the knowledge graph is that schema populated with real data. Ontology = the type system and rules. Knowledge graph = the ontology plus millions of actual instances (this customer, that order) as a queryable graph. As the field puts it: a semantic layer is for lookup; an ontology is for context and reasoning.

The languages

RDF — the base data model: every fact is a subject-predicate-object triple ("order-123 → placed-by → customer-7"). RDFS / OWL — OWL (Web Ontology Language) sits on top of RDF and adds classes, relationships, and logical constraints rich enough for a machine to infer new knowledge. SPARQL — the query language for triples, the graph world's answer to SQL. If you see .ttl files (Turtle), that's RDF/OWL being authored by hand.

The one that's different

Palantir is the odd one out and worth holding separate. Its "Ontology" is operational — objects plus actions plus writeback plus simulation, a decision-and-transaction layer. That's a genuinely sold category, but a heavier, different job than "ground my analytics agent in the right definitions." When a competitor takes a data-modeling deal out on "ontology," it's usually selling that operational, writeback-and-actions story — not the metrics-grounding story a semantic layer plays in. Keep the two straight and you won't get bulldozed by the word.

The pragmatic ladder for a warehouse-native team: your dbt project (models + semantic models + relationships tests + descriptions) is already a lightweight, working ontology in git. The formal stack above — Protégé to author, a triple store to reason, OWL to express rules — is where you go when plain-text relationships stop being enough and you need typed inference. Most teams aren't at that line yet. That's why "can the semantic layer be our ontology?" is a live question, not a settled no.

13. Three vendors, three bets: dbt · Databricks · Palantir

"Ontology" is on every vendor's slide now, meaning something different each time. Two real product diagrams make the split obvious.

Databricks (Genie) stacks two middle pillars: Unity Catalog Semantics — the human-authored modeled semantics (metrics, glossary, domains) — next to a Genie Knowledge Store of machine-inferred knowledge (expressions people actually write, concepts, who the experts are). It fuses them into a "continuously learned" ontology that serves agents and dashboards. The bet: your ontology is authored semantics plus what the platform learns from how you really query. It's analytical — it stops at answering. No action layer.

Palantir (Foundry) draws three layers: a Semantic layer ("what the world is" — entities and relationships, person owns car), a Kinetic layer ("connecting to reality" — binding those types to live data), and a Dynamic layer ("making it come alive" — actions). The bet: an ontology isn't just knowledge, it's operational — it reaches all the way to doing and writing back to source systems. Palantir's ontology decides and acts; Databricks' answers.

dbt is the authored, governed semantic core — the left pillar of the Databricks picture, done git-native and warehouse-portable. Its bet: trust comes from version-controlled definitions, and the ontology/agent story extends from that (descriptions, relationships, a metrics registry, MCP) rather than from a separate learned store or an action layer. dbt owns the authored rung and wires it straight to agents; it doesn't ship the inferred-knowledge store or the writeback layer.

The disambiguation, one line each

dbt — authored + git-governed. Ontology = an extension of the semantic layer you already trust.
Databricks Genie — modeled semantics + machine-learned context, fused. Ontology = trusted definitions plus what the platform infers from usage.
Palantir Foundry — types → live data → actions. Ontology = an operating layer that decides and writes back, not just a map.

One honest caveat: dbt Labs hasn't shipped a single "ontology" diagram like the other two, and the platform naming is mid-transition — so the dbt panel above is a construction of where its pieces sit, not an official product picture. The vendor diagrams are their own marketing; the mapping onto a shared frame is the useful part.

The cheat-sheet answer

"I already have refs — why a semantic layer?"

"Because refs make your tables trustworthy, and a metric isn't a table — it's an aggregation across dimensions chosen at query time. The moment anyone groups or joins your trusted model they're writing SQL you never reviewed, and two specific things break: fan-out silently inflates sums across one-to-many joins, and ratios can't be averaged back together. The semantic layer sits on your existing tables — one YAML description per model, building nothing — and defines each metric once: grain, join paths, numerator/denominator. Then it generates correct SQL on demand, the same way for Looker, Hex, and your agents. It's not more trust in the model. It's trust that survives being queried, and one definition instead of the same metric re-built in four tools that quietly disagree."

And the ontology follow-on: the semantic layer is the executable core of an ontology — the metrics and the relationships needed to compute them. If a team wants a full reasoning map for AI, what their evaluation is really deciding is whether the semantic layer covers enough of that map to be the ontology, or whether they'll bolt a separate one alongside it. For most teams today, that map can start as an extension of the same git project — which is why it's a question worth engaging, not conceding.
